Where a model becomes a part.

A battery pack is a 3D model long before it's a physical object. We sat down with one of our mechanical design engineers to talk through what happens between the drawing and the finished part.

What does a mechanical design engineer actually own on a project?

Everything that has to physically fit together — enclosure, mounting, thermal paths, how the electronics and cells sit relative to each other. If two things can't be in the same place at the same time, that's a mechanical design problem.

How much of the day is spent in CAD versus anywhere else?

Less than people assume. A model is only useful once it's been checked against the drawing, the tolerances, and eventually the physical part. A lot of the real work happens comparing what's on screen to what's actually in front of you.

Where do design problems usually surface?

At the interfaces — where a bracket meets a cell, where a connector has to reach through a wall it wasn't drawn to reach through. Nothing fails in isolation; it fails against a neighbouring part.

How do you know a design is manufacturable, not just correct on screen?

By building it, or getting as close to that as early as possible. A first article gets measured against the model, not just eyeballed, and any gap between the two gets resolved before it becomes a production problem.

What's satisfying about this part of the job?

Watching an exploded assembly on a screen become an actual pack you can pick up and hold, with every part landing exactly where the drawing said it would.
